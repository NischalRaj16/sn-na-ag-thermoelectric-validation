The inefﬁcient carrier transport of earth-abundant tin sulﬁde (SnS), arising
from its intrinsically low crystal symmetry, limits its thermoelectric perfor-
mance at low temperatures. Here, the substitutional Ag+ doping modiﬁes the
Na-doped SnS crystal structure by reinforcing lattice dynamical anisotropy
and enhancing in-plane transport properties. Concurrently, the reconstructed
valence-band structure evolves into a modulated multiband conﬁguration,
increasing the density-of-states effective mass while preserving high carrier
mobility. The resulting highly metallic conductivity combined with high
thermopower yields the room-temperature power factor of ~101µWc m−1K−2 in
Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S single crystals. Coupled with the intrinsically low thermal
conductivity of SnS, the optimized crystal achieves the ﬁgure of merit (zT)o f
~1.06 at 300 K and ~2.0 at 573 K, delivering a conversion efﬁciency of ~3.3% and
an output power density of ~0.31 W cm−2 in a single-leg module under a 243 K
temperature gradient. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of crystal
structure modiﬁcation and multiband engineering in SnS and establish it as a
highly promising low-cost thermoelectric material for near-room-temperature
energy conversion.
Thermoelectric (TE) devices offer a unique solid-state route for heat-
to-electricity conversion, enabling compact and maintenance-free
energy harvesting via the Seebeck effect. Conversely, the Peltier effect
provides on-chip and electronics cooling without the need for
refrigerant1,2. Despite these inherent advantages, the widespread
deployment of thermoelectrics has been hindered by the sluggish
progress in developing the conversion efﬁciency of the semiconductor
materials, which is determined by the dimensionless ﬁgure of merit,
zT = σS2T/(κl+κe), where T i st h ea b s o l u t et e m p e r a t u r e3,4.T h et h r e e
transport parameters, electrical conductivity (σ), Seebeck coefﬁcient
(S), and the thermal conductivity from the electronic part ( κe), are
strongly interdependent, making the optimization challenging.
Therefore, gainingzT typically focuses on minimizing the independent
variable of lattice thermal conductivityκl 5, while simultaneously max-
imizing the power factor (PF = σS2)6,7.

operating near room temperature (RT) have intensiﬁed, as evidenced

In this regard, layered tin chalcogenides, including tin selenide
(SnSe)17–19 and tin sulﬁde (SnS)20,21, have emerged as highly attractive
candidates delivering highzT values for low to mid-temperatures. SnS,
while structurally analogous, offers practical advantages, including
lower toxicity, lower cost, and greater elemental abundance20,21.A l i o -
valent doping by substitutional cations (Na+ and Ag+) has been proven
effective for tailoring the carrier density and enhancing p-type TE
efﬁciency22–25. On the other hand, isovalent doping at the S site by Se
promotes the enhancement ofPF through band structure modiﬁcation
and κl reduction by lowering the phonon group velocity26,27. However,
in conventional polycrystalline SnS, the peak TE performance typically
appears at elevated temperatures, mainly due to strong carrier scat-
tering at grain boundaries that limits carrier mobility (μ) near RT. This
effect is further reinforced by the structural phase transition from the
low-symmetry Pnma phase to the high-symmetry Cmcm phase at high
temperature, which enhances μ and shifts optimal performance
toward higher temperatures 25,27. In contrast, the single-crystalline
counterpart eliminates grain-boundary scattering and exposes the
intrinsic transport behavior of the lattice, enabling superior TE per-
formance near RT. For example, SnS0.9Se0.1, optimized by controlled
sulfur enrichment through SnS2 addition, exhibits a power factor of
~58 μWc m −1 K−2 at 300 K and reaches a peak zT of ~1.6 at 573 K 28.
However, these strategies, which primarily relied on increasing the
carrier concentration (n), inevitably compromisedμ due to Sn vacan-
cies, thereby limiting further advancement near RT.
This limitation motivated us to implement an aliovalent co-doping
strategy in single-crystalline SnS that preserves efﬁcient carrier trans-
port even at elevated n, owing to valence-band reconstruction. Na⁺ is
ﬁrst introduced in this SnS to regulate the hole concentration, pro-
viding a direct handle on band degeneracy. As illustrated in Fig. 1a,
pristine SnS exhibits four valence bands (VBs) distributed along the
Y–Γ–Z–U directions in momentum space, with their maxima located
relatively close to the Fermi level. However, n achieved by Na doping
alone is insufﬁcient to activate all VBs for multi-valley transport, due to
the relatively large band offset (~0.1 eV≈ 4 kBT). To address this, Ag⁺ is
introduced as a secondary dopant to further engineer the valence-
band structure.
Higher Ag concentrations enhance the band evolution, where VB3
begins to converge and merge with VB1 into a single dispersive feature,
as conﬁrmed by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES).
Qin et al. reported a similar phenomenon in Pb-alloyed SnSe, known as
a “pudding-mold” band structure arising from multiband synglisis 18.
While similar modulation has been demonstrated in SnSe, this work
presents an effective strategy for valence-band reconstruction in
lower-cost, more environmentally friendly SnS single crystals29. Addi-
tionally, the merging band ﬂattens the valence-band maximum,
enhancing the effective mass ( m⁎) while preserving high μ by sup-
pressing intervalley scattering, with additionalμ gains arising from the
convergence of the light band (VB3) at the Y –Zv a l l e y s19,27. The band
convergence, arising from crystal structure modi ﬁcation, is accom-
panied by a reduced deformation potential ( Ξ), enabling ef ﬁcient
carrier transport even within a ﬂattened band structure. This

Results and Discussion
Enhanced TE transport properties
Figure 2 summarizes the TE transport properties measured along the
crystallographic b-axis, which exhibits the highest performance in this
system20,21. High-quality single crystals were synthesized using a
modiﬁed Bridgman method and subsequently cut into well-de ﬁned
geometries for transport measurements (see Supplementary Fig. 1).
Phase conﬁrmation and crystallographic orientation were conﬁrmed
by X-ray diffraction and Laue back-reﬂection analysis (Supplementary
Figs. 2 and 3), which veri ﬁed the orthorhombic Pnma structure and
accurately resolved the principal crystal axes. Ag incorporation indu-
ces a pronounced evolution ofS (Fig. 2a), which increases from ~224 to
~246 µVK −1 at RT as Ag concentration rises from 0 to 0.04, despite the
simultaneous rise in measured nH.M o r e o v e r ,σ at RT improved gra-
dually up to ~1700 S cm −1 for x =0 . 0 3 ( F i g .2b), which is the highest
value reported to date among SnS single crystals 27,28. The modest
decrease ofσ with temperature reﬂects the expected acoustic-phonon
transport, consistent with T−1.5 behavior of μw s h o w ni nF i g .2bi n s e t .
Despite the large Ag nominal (x) in this compound, the positive trend
of σ and nH conﬁrmed the successful cationic substitution in the
crystals. However, with further increase in Ag concentration, Ag
aggregation becomes more pronounced, as revealed by the elemental
mapping results (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5 and Supplementary
Table 1). In particular, microscale Ag-rich regions become clearly
observable forx = 0.04, leading to reductions in bothnH and μw, likely
due to the reduced ef ﬁciency of electrically active Ag acceptors with
enhanced carrier scattering induced by the aggregated regions.
Meanwhile, Agx Sny -related impurity phases are barely detectable in
the synchrotron radiation powder X-ray diffraction (SR-PXRD) pat-
terns (Supplementary Fig. 6). The possible SnS 2 secondary phase
associated with Sn deﬁciency coincides with the main (400) diffraction
peak of SnS, making its identi ﬁcation challenging. Overall, the sub-
stantial improvement of σ and S resulted in the enhancement of PF
through this Ag incorporation (Fig. 2c). Consequently, the maximum
PF reached ~101 μWc m−1 K−2 at 300 K for x = 0.03, a value essentially
higher than the high performance reported for the SnS and SnSe single
crystal18,28.

With increasing Ag concentration, all four Raman-active modes exhibit
a systematic red shift, indicating phonon softening associated with the
progressive introduction of chemical defects32,33. Notably, the in-plane
vibrational modes display only slight softening, whereas the red shifts
of the out-of-plane modes are more pronounced. This anisotropic
phonon renormalization reﬂects enhanced vibrational anisotropy at
higher Ag content, characterized by a stronger in-plane vibrational
component. Such behavior is consistent with the observed structural
distortions and is expected to reduce the phonon mean free path,
thereby suppressing the κₗ while preserving favorable electrical
transport properties34–36.
In addition, microstructural analysis of the Sn 0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S
crystal by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) reveals
local structural features associated with lattice modiﬁcation induced
by Ag incorporation (Fig.4a–c). High-angle annular dark-ﬁeld (HAADF)
STEM imaging at higher magniﬁcation clearly shows the displacement
of Sn atoms (Fig. 4b), consistent with the systematic evolution of the
Sn–Sb o n da n g l eθ and the accompanying lattice modulation. Atomic-
resolution imaging in Fig. 4c further identiﬁes dislocation cores and
locally distorted Sn –S coordination environments, which are corro-
borated by elemental mapping using electron energy-loss spectro-
scopy (EELS). The Sn atomic displacements resolved by EELS provide
direct structural evidence for lattice reconstruction in Ag-doped SnS.
Such distortions are expected to introduce spatialﬂuctuations in local
strain gradients, leading to systematic deviations ofθ in layered crystal
systems37,38. Collectively, these ordered defect motifs are hypothesized
to shorten the phonon mean free path and suppress the κₗ35,i n
agreement with the Raman-observed phonon softening and the con-
currently preserved high electrical conductivity upon Ag
incorporation.
Electronic band structure measurement via ARPES
ARPES measurements were performed at RT using a photon energy of
92 eV to experimentally probe the evolution of the valence-band
structure in Sn0.99−xNa0.01AgxS for selected compositions (x =0 ,0 . 0 1 ,
and 0.03), as shown in Fig. 5a, b. The energy dispersion (E)a l o n gt h e
Γ–Zm o m e n t u mp a t h(k) was resolved to examine the ﬁrst valence-
band maximum (VBM1), which predominantly governs the electrical
transport properties of SnS (Fig.5a). For all compositions, the VBM lies
close to the Fermi energy ( EF), indicating that SnS is already in a
degenerate regime. Upon the introd uction of substitutional Ag, the
valence bands become more clearly distinguishable, revealing a pro-
nounced evolution of the band structure. Along the Γ–Z k-path,
another light band appears at relatively lower binding energy (around
−0.2 eV from EF), here labeled as VB3 (based on the schematic in
Fig. 1a). The corresponding energy distribution curve (EDC) analysis is
provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. This band is separated from VB1 by
approximately 0.2 Å−1 in k-space. Once Ag is introduced, VB3 begins to
converge, especially forx = 0.03, where it approaches close toEF with a
signiﬁcantly reduced band offset (ΔE) between VBM1 and VBM3. This
evolution shifts VBM1 away from the Z-point (by ~0.06 Å−1), conﬁrming
the k-space alignment of this extremum and indicating the formation
of a pudding-mold–type band structure18. Such band convergence is
expected to enhance the effective valence-band degeneracy (Nv). The
noticeable bandﬂattening results in an increased band effective mass
(m∗/me), particularly at higher Ag concentrations. Byﬁtting the second
derivative of the ARPES intensity maps using the E(k) dispersion rela-
tion (see Eq. (1)i nM e t h o d s ) ,m∗/me values were extracted for different
Ag contents (see Supplementary Table 6). The Na-doped SnS sample
(x =0 ) e x h i b i t sm∗/me ≈ 0.34 ± 0.02, which increases to 0.45 ± 0.08 in
Sn0.96 Na0.01Ag0.03S at VBM1. Consequently, band ﬂattening together
with enhanced degeneracy (Nv) effectively increasesS, consistent with
the Pisarenko plot of nH vs S based on the single parabolic band (SPB)
model (Supplementary Fig. 15). In this plot, Ag-rich samples fall along
the theoretical line corresponding to a larger DOS effective mass
(md*= Nv 2/3m*) of 1.97me. This behavior agrees with the Mott relation39
S ∝ md*Tn-2/3, which indicates that a moderate increase in nH does not
suppress S due to concurrent enhancement of md*.

Multivalley transport simulation
To correlate the experimentally measured transport properties with
the band modulation observed by ARPES and DFT, we analyzed the
relationships among S, Hall carrier concentration ( nH), and Hall
mobility (μH). The μH values were calculated using Eq. (3.15), with the
Hall coef ﬁcients provided in Supplementary Fig. 20. Multiple-band
transport simulations based on the acoustic deformation potential
(ADP) model40,41 were performed to compare the electrical transport
behavior of pristine SnS with that of a three-band conﬁguration. In this
model, the three bands comprise VB(1 + 3), VB2, and VB4, whereas
pristine SnS contains four separated valence bands with expanded
energy offsets ( ΔE) for VB3 and VB4, as schematically illustrated in
Fig. 1a. Details of the calculations are provided in the Supplementary
Methods, while the relevant parameters, including ΔE, m*, and Ξ in
Supplementary Tables 7 and 9 –11. Further discussions regarding the
valence-band (VB) coordinates and the principal m*a r ep r e s e n t e di n
the Supplementary Discussion.
As shown in Fig. 7a, the experimental S of the Ag-doped samples
closely follows the curve corresponding to the modulated three-band
conﬁguration, indicating that the enhanced S originates from band
ﬂattening associated with the convergence between VB1 and VB3. The
introduction of Ag also substantially increases μH, bringing the mea-
sured values into good agreement with the predictions of the modu-
lated multiband model, as shown in Fig.7b. The reduced energy offset
(ΔE) between the two extrema (VB1 and VB4) along the Y–Γ–Zd i r e c t i o n
increases the contribution of the light-transport valley (VB4), which
possesses a lower Ξ, resulting in a maximum μH of of ~327 cm² V⁻¹s ⁻¹
in Sn 0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S. In addition, Boltzmann transport calculations
(Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22) further support the experimentally
observed Ag-dependent transport evolution (see Supporting Methods
for details). Although the calculated conductivity and mobility are
lower than the corresponding experimental values because of uncer-
tainties in the state-dependent relaxation-time model and the dis-
tinction between calculated drift mobility and measured Hall mobility,
the calculations reproduce the key experimental trend: Ag-induced
band reconstruction enhances carrier transport relative tox =0 , w i t h
x = 0.03 providing the optimum balance betweenσ and S.
To explain the high μH in this material, the valence-band edge of
SnS exhibits a highly anisotropic electronic structure, as revealed by
the constant-energy contours and three-dimensional Fermi surfaces
(Supplementary Figs. 23–27). The hole pocket is strongly dispersive
along the ( ky) direction, relatively ﬂat along ( kx), and non-parabolic
along (kz), resulting in anisotropic effective masses and non-spherical
carrier pockets. Such an anisotropic band topology is advantageous
for thermoelectric transport because the ﬂatter directions enhance
md*a n d S, while the more dispersive directions preserve ef ﬁcient
carrier transport. Together with the increasednH and activation of the
light bands (VB3 and VB4), this anisotropic multivalley electronic
structure provides a plausible origin for the simultaneous enhance-
ment of m*a n dμH (see the supplementary discussion for the details of
band anisotropy and degeneracy).

In summary, the distorted crystal structure associated with the
bond angle (θ) in SnS governs the transport physics over a wide tem-
perature range, and reconstructing this structural motif enables highly
metallic transport that bene ﬁts low-temperature TE performance.
Upon structural modiﬁcation through Ag incorporation, multivalence-
band convergence facilitated ef ﬁcient charge transport in ﬂattened
bands with a low Ξ, preserving high μ while increasing m*. Conse-
quently, the strong coupling effect between σ and S was mitigated in
Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S single crystal, enabling the realization of a highPF
of ~101 µWc m−1 K−2 at RT. In this study, the aliovalent co-doping strat-
egy with Na ⁺ and Ag⁺ successfully established an emerging platform
for SnS-based crystals, in which structural modulation directly gave
rise to favorable TE properties. Notably, a high zT peak of ~2 at 573 K
was realized within a sul ﬁde-based system, highlighting a signi ﬁcant
advance in earth-abundant and environmentally friendly thermo-
electrics near RT.
Methods
Single crystal synthesis
Puriﬁed Sn pellets (99.99 wt.% pure) and Na ingot (99.99 wt.%
pure) from Ultimate Materials Technology, S powder (99.98 wt.%
pure) from Sigma-Aldrich, and Ag powder (99.999 wt.% pure)
from Thermo ﬁscher Puratronics were weighed carefully and cal-
culated according to the stoichiometry of (Ag or x =0 ,1 ,2 ,3 ,a n d
4a t .% )S n 0.99-xNa0.01AgxS. All weighted precursors were prepared
inside the carbon-coated quartz tube and sealed under ~50 mTorr
vacuum with an outer larger quartz tube to avoid oxidation from
the high-temperature synthesis inside the furnace. The mixed
materials were annealed to 1273 K using a slow heating ramp and
held at this temperature for 3 days. Subsequently, the samples
were cooled to room temperature inside the furnace. The
resulting polycrystalline SnS ingots were crushed, ground, and
sieved through a 200-mesh screen to ensure compositional uni-
formity. Approximately 20 g SnS ﬁne powder was then trans-
ferred into a 13 mm inner-diameter carbon-coated conical quartz
tube and double-sealed with a larger outer quartz tube. For
single-crystal growth, the sealed samples were placed in a vertical
furnace. The sample was held in the hot zone and heated to
1273 K for 24 h, then maintained at this temperature for an addi-
tional 2 days to ensure complete melting, followed by transfer to
the cold zone at 1073 K using a pulling parameter corresponding
to a cooling rate of 1 K h −1. The entire single-crystal growth pro-
cess required approximately 14 days to obtain a crystal with a
length of ~5 cm. After growth, the crystals were naturally cooled
to room temperature inside the furnace for 1 day.
Material characterizations
The phase purity of the as-synthesized crystals was characterized using
powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) performed on a Bruker D2 dif-
fractometer equipped with Cu K α radiation ( λ = 1.5406 Å). Crystal
orientation was examined using electron backscattered diffraction
(EBSD) combined with ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscopy
(FESEM; JEOL JSM-7800F) and Laue X-ray diffraction. High-angle
annular dark-ﬁeld scanning transmission electron microscopy com-
bined with high-resolution electron energy-loss spectroscopy (HAADF-
STEM-EELS) was performed using a JEOL JEM −2100F transmission
electron microscope equipped with aberration correction. STEM spe-
cimens were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) milling at iTek
Technology Co., Ltd.
Raman spectroscopy and ﬁtting procedure
Raman spectra were measured using a custom-built confocal ﬁber
microscope integrated with a HORIBA iHR550 spectrometer, equipped
with a Syncerity BI-NIR CCD camera (wavelength range: 400–1075 nm)
and a 532 nm diode laser as the excitation source. The scattered signals
were collected using a photomultiplier detector (PicoQuant PMA192;
wavelength range: 230–920 nm). Measurements were performed on
the basal plane of small SnS bulk crystals with dimensions of
approximately 4 × 4 mm 2 in size. For each sample, spectra were
acquired from up to ﬁve different locations with an exposure time of
approximately 90 s per spot. The ﬁnal Raman spectrum was obtained
by averaging the spectra collected from different spots. Raman peak
positions were determined by Gaussian ﬁtting using OriginLab soft-
ware, yielding a ﬁtting quality with residuals corresponding
to R² ≈ 0.99.
Synchrotron radiation measurements
Most synchrotron-based experiments were carried out at the
Taiwan Photon Source (TPS) of the National Synchrotron Radia-
tion Research Center (NSRRC), Taiwan. High-energy (20 keV)
synchrotron X-ray diffraction was used to collect powder dif-
fraction data. For Rietveld re ﬁnement, crystal powders were
prepared by hand grinding and sieved through a 400-mesh
screen to ensure uniform particle size for all samples. For ele-
mental mapping, bulk single-crystal specimens were analyzed
using nano-focused X-ray ﬂuorescence (XRF) at TPS beamline 21 A
with a polychromatic X-ray source covering an energy range of
5–30 keV. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements at
the Sn K-edge were conducted at TPS beamline 44A1 in trans-
mission mode using ground SnS powder as the specimen. An
energy scan operating in step mode was employed to acquire
optimized absorption spectra at the Ag K-edge in ﬂuorescence
detection.
