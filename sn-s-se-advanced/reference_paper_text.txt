ABSTRACT
Thermoelectric materials are crucial for energy conversion and waste heat recovery. However, materials design 
based on First-principles calculation is computationally expensive. This study proposes an interpretable machine 
learning approach to accelerate the screening of novel thermoelectric materials. A dataset of 1093 samples was 
constructed, and 132 feature parameters were extracted. A model framework integrating a composite feature 
selection strategy with six machine learning algorithms was developed. As the most effective model, XGBoost 
was chosen for predicting material performance. Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis identified key 
features, and a two-dimensional material, SnSe0.25Te0.75, was designed with a 16 % enhancement in thermo-
electric performance, highly consistent with the First-principles calculation. The study provides an effective 
pathway for exploring and optimizing thermoelectric materials.
1. Introduction
As global energy demand rises and shortages worsen, sustainable 
strategies focusing on conservation and emission reduction have become 
crucial. About 66 % of energy is wasted as heat in industry, transport, 
and electronics due to low energy efficiency [1–3]. The market for waste 
heat power generation and recovery is expected to grow. Enhancing 
energy conversion efficiency, especially in thermoelectric materials, is a 
key focus [4,5]. Thermoelectric devices, which convert heat to elec-
tricity [6], are more efficient than traditional converters. They are 
compact, reliable, quiet, and eco-friendly, making them ideal for auto-
motive, aerospace, medical electronics, and wearables [7,8]. Developing 
new thermoelectric materials with high efficiency and stability is crucial 
for better energy utilization [9].
Traditional thermoelectric materials with precious metal electrodes 
are expensive to prepare, have a long testing cycle (over 72 h) for a 
single sample, and are inefficient to develop. Computer technology, 
combined with density functional theory (DFT), local density approxi-
mation (LDA), and generalized gradient approximation (GGA), can 
calculate the energy bands and photovoltaic properties based on the 
material’s crystal structure [10,11], thus enhancing development effi-
ciency. However, DFT methods still encounter the technical bottleneck 
of exponentially increasing computational complexity when analyzing 
complex energy band structures and multiscale transport processes 
[12–15]. The Boltzmann transport equation, molecular dynamics (MD) 
simulations, and the Debye-Callaway model provide crucial support for 
comprehending the transport properties of materials [16,17]. Machine 
learning models can identify potential laws from complex data [18–20], 
achieve the resolution of multidimensional properties and performance 
prediction of materials, and improving the discovery efficiency and 
performance evaluation accuracy of new thermoelectric materials 
[21–23].
GyoungS. Na et al. [24] proposed a Systematic Identification of 
Materials Descriptor (SIMD). This method classifies materials into 
clusters based on their physical and chemical properties, then fuses the 
system vectors of these clusters with the target statistic to train machine 
learning model. Consequently, the prediction accuracy of the model was 
significantly improved. Xue Jia et al. [25] screened 456 half-Heusler 
compounds from the Materials Project database. They selected 20 sta-
ble thermoelectric materials from 61 candidates through iterative 
screening using seven clustering algorithms based on thermoelectric 
performance metrics. U.S. Vaitesswar et al. [26] established a joint 
prediction framework combining Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CGCNN) and Random Forest (RF). This framework was 
validated by First-principles calculation and successfully identified four 
novel half-Heusler compounds, TiGePt, ZrInAu, ZrSiPd, and ZrSiPt.

Given the limitations of prior studies, such as the low accuracy of 
thermoelectric property predictions, the insufficient interpretability of 
machine learning models, and the failure to validate material’s stability 
under high-temperature conditions, we collected 1093 thermoelectric 
materials from open-source databases and relevant literature. Using 
Matminer, we extracted features from these materials, and applied 
several machine learning techniques to predict their thermoelectric 
power factor (PF) and thermal conductivity (TC). We designed a com-
posite feature selection method to select the best features and intro-
duced the SHapley additive exPlanations method [27] to interpret the 
constructed model. Finally, a high ZT-value thermoelectric material 
design method is proposed, and the feasibility of the method is verified 
by First-principles calculation [28].
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data collection and preprocessing
The thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT = PF*T/TC) is a basic index 
for evaluating the performance of thermoelectric materials. Optimizing 
the thermoelectric power factor (PF) and thermal conductivity (TC) is 
the key to improving the thermoelectric properties of materials. In this 
study, we integrate the UCSB (University of California, Santa Barbara) 
thermoelectric database [29] and the literature [24] to construct 1093 
standardized samples comprising 64 elements. Each sample covers the 
parameters of the material’s chemical formula, test temperature, See-
beck coefficient, thermal conductivity TC, power factor PF, and ther-
moelectric optimum value ZT. In this paper, PF and TC are used as the 
prediction targets, and the specific data are shown in the Supplementary 
Materials Table S1. As shown in Fig. 1, the power factor and thermal 
conductivity parameters exhibibit right-skewed distributions, and a 
significant class imbalance is observed. This necessitates the use of 
stratified sampling during model training and the incorporation of a 
weighted loss function to enhance model robustness.

2.3. Feature selection
In machine learning, redundant features can induce feature catas-
trophe [41], which hampers model training. Therefore, the irrelevant or 
redundant features are generally eliminated by feature selection to find 
the optimal subset of features, which reduces the feature dimension 
while improving the accuracy of the model. In this paper, feature se-
lection is accomplished by variance threshold selection, recursive 
feature elimination, Monte Carlo simulation, and Pearson’s correlation 
coefficient, as shown in Fig. 2.
2.3.1. Variance threshold feature selection
The variance thresholding method is used to initially screen the 
original features and construct a first-generation feature subset [42]. 
The variability of feature values is quantified by calculating the feature 
variance Vx=E[(x-μx)2]. Features with variance exceeding a predefined 
threshold are retained. High variance indicates significant data volatility 
and stronger explanatory power for the target variable. Conversely, low 
variance indicates less volatility in the data and less impact on the 
model, and thus is eliminated. The process effectively removed static 
redundant parameters and selected 137 features out of the initial 184.
2.3.2. Recursive feature elimination
The XGB model is chosen as the base estimator for recursive feature 
elimination. The base estimator is trained on the initial features set and 
assigns a weight to each feature, then removes the least important 
feature from the current set and repeats this process iteratively until the 
desired number of features is reached.
2.3.3. Monte Carlo simulation feature selection
After the recursive feature selection is completed, to improve the 
efficiency of multidimensional feature combination optimization, we 
adopt the Monte Carlo simulation technique based on probabilistic 
statistics to stably evaluate the model performance under different 
feature combinations by randomly sampling the candidate features and 
repeating the experiments, and retain the feature combinations with 
high R2 values. This method improves the accuracy of the model, re-
duces the computational complexity, and achieves high training effi-
ciency while ensuring the robustness of the model [43,44].
2.3.4. Pearson correlation analysis
Pearson correlation analysis is utilized to finalized the feature se-
lection. The correlation coefficients (as shown in Eq. 1), ranging from 
  1–1, indicate the strength and direction of the liner relationship be-
tween features, where   1 and 1 represent perfect negative and positive 
correlations, respectively. In the specific implementation, the correla-
tion coefficients between all features two by two are calculated first, and 
then a specific threshold is set so that when the absolute value of the 
correlation coefficients of two or more features exceeds the threshold, 
only one of the features is retained [45].

2.4. Machine learning algorithms and evaluation metrics

analysis, including Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting 
(XGBoost), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Decision Tree (DT), Support 
Vector Machine Regression (SVR), and Ridge Regression (Ridge) algo-
rithms. Both Ridge regression and Support Vector Machine Regression 
(SVR), which are commonly used in regression tasks, are considered, 
and Decision Trees and their integration methods are also covered, while 
neural network models are introduced to enhance prediction 
performance.
RF [46] is an ensemble learning method based on the Bagging 
framework with CART trees, which improves the accuracy of the model 
by integrating the prediction results from multiple decision trees. MLP 
[47] employs a feed-forward neural network architecture with a hier-
archical neuron structure to achieve nonlinear interactions between 
features. XGBoost [48] uses a stepwise increase in decision trees to 
improve model performance by optimizing feature splitting nodes. DT 
[49] simulates the decision-making process by constructing a tree 
structure with a hierarchical structure, thus effectively accomplishing 
the prediction task. SVR [50], which employs an ε-insensitive loss 
function as an optimization criterion to regularize the constraints on 
deviations beyond a preset threshold. Ridge regression [51], based on 
traditional linear regression, introduces an L2 regularization term to 
address the limitations of ordinary least squares in multiple covariance 
and overfitting scenarios, and maintains the stability of the model in the

2.5. Model implementation
In this study, a randomized partitioning strategy was used to allocate 
1093 data in the ratio of 8:2 between the training set (874) and the test 
set (219) for model training and performance evaluation, respectively. 
In order to eliminate the effect of feature magnitude differences on the 
model, normalization of input features was implemented before model 
training [52]. The feature data were made to conform to a standard 
normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1 by z-score standardi-
zation method, and the transformation formula is shown in Eq. 5, where 
x denotes the original feature value, x′ is the standardized feature value, 
and μ and σ correspond to the feature mean and standard deviation of 
the training data, respectively. 
xʹ= x  μ σ (5) 
To minimize the random bias caused by data insufficiency in the 
partitioning process, this study employed the K-fold cross-validation 
method (K = 10) to optimize the model’s stability [53], and the 
optimal hyperparameters were determined using the grid search 
method.

pseudopotentials used are based on the generalized-gradient approxi-
mation with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE) exchange-correlation

For the electronic thermal conductivity (κe), the crystal structure was 
first self-consistent with high precision (K-resolved = 0.03), and then the 
correlation data obtained from BoltzTraP calculations were used to 
obtain κe, κe = κ0   TσS2, where κ0 was calculated by BoltzTraP and is 
the electronic thermal conductivity under closed-circuit conditions, T is 
the temperature, σ is the conductivity, and S is the Seebeck coefficient 
[57,58]. κph is calculated by solving the linearized phonon Boltzmann 
transport equation (LBTE) using the ShengBTE software package, which 
takes into account the 3rd-order force constants using an iterative 
method with n-grid = 21× 21× 1 [59]. Specifically, the SnSe0.25Te0.75 
protocell has 8 atoms, and we perform a 4× 4× 1 cell expansion for the 
high-precision optimized structure, and the second-order force constant 
matrix is obtained by the finite-displacement method in combination 
with VASP and phynopy calculations. The optimized structure is sub-
jected to 3× 3× 1 cell expansion, the forces of 14 neighboring atoms 
are considered, and the single-point energies of 2588 POSCARs are 
calculated, and the third-order force constant matrices are calculated by 
combining with Thirdorder [60,61], and the lattice thermal conductiv-
ity κph is calculated based on the second-order and the third-order force 
constant matrices by ShengBTE.
The phonon dispersion was obtained by performing density- 
functional perturbation theory (DFPT) as implemented in the Phonopy 
software package. To evaluate the thermal stability of the SnSe0.25Te0.75 
monolayer, ab initio computational molecular dynamics (AIMD) simu-
lations were carried out on a 4× 4× 1 supercell, using canonical 
ensemble (NVT) with a Nos´e-Hoover thermal bath scheme [62], setting 
a time step of 1 fs, a setup step of 9000 steps, and a total simulation 
duration of 9 ps.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Evaluation of models based on original features
The performance of six machine learning algorithms was tested 
based on the original features, and their hyperparameters were opti-
mized using grid search method, the specific information of which is 
given in Table S2 of the Supplementary Materials. Fig. 3 shows the 
training visualization graphs of 10-fold cross-validation for the six 
models, the black dashed line indicates the perfect model fitting state, 
the blue line indicates the fitting curve of the model predicted values to 
the actual values under the training set data, and the red line indicates 
the fitting curve of the model predicted values to the actual values under

better performance in predicting PF values (R2 = 0.818, MAE = 2.8×
10  4, RMSE = 4.6× 10  4), (R2 = 0.858, MAE = 2.1× 10  4, RMSE =

3.3. Performance of the XGB model based on optimized parameters
The model is trained by the optimal feature subset, and its learning 
rate-R2 curve is shown in Fig. 7. Firstly, the random_state parameter of 
the XGB model is analyzed, and with n_estimators kept at the default 
value of 100, as shown in Fig. 7a, when the value of random_state is 
taken to be 22, the R2 values of both PF and TC reach the peak, where PF 
is 0.854 and TC is 0.95, which indicates that setting the random_state set 
to 22 is optimal. Further observation of Fig. 7b reveals that for PF, the R2 
value rises to 0.842 when the number of iterations reaches 10, and then 
the growth of the R2 value tends to level off with the increase of 
n_estimator; for TC, the R2 value stabilizes at 0.95 when the number of 
iterations reaches 30, and then shows the same smooth trend.
Comparing the distribution of predicted and actual values by visual 
means, Fig. 8 demonstrates the deviation of prediction results from the 
ideal fit line (red dashed line). The proximity of the predicted points to 
this line reflects the model’s prediction accuracy. As shown in Fig. 8a, 
when the XGB model is trained on PF data, the test set an R2 of 0.86, an 
MAE of 0.00024, and an RMSE of 0.00044. In Fig. 8b, when the TC data 
is trained, the R2 is 0.94, the MAE is 0.41, and the RMSE is 1.16.
It can be seen that in the high-value regions of PF and TC, some of the 
predicted points exhibit significant deviations from the true values, 
likely due to insufficient sample size in these regions. To enhance the 
model’s prediction accuracy, it is recommended to increase the sample 
size in future studies, especially for the data samples with high PF and 
TC values.

3.5. Thermoelectric material design methodology
Based on the results of the XGB model and the analysis of the SHAP 
value feature suggested values, we propose a strategy to optimize the 
thermoelectric properties. Taking the two materials Ni2Mo6S8 and 
Bi88Sb12 as an example, the calculation of the key feature values of the 
two materials is shown in Fig. 11, in which the grey shaded area is 
visually labeled on the SHAP-recommended feature value intervals. 
Only three of the eight features (M_range_n, D_std_v, M_mean_e) of 
Ni2Mo6S8 fall within the range of SHAP value feature recommended 
values. The other five features fall outside the SHAP value feature sug-
gestion value. This means that the PF value for Ni2Mo6S8 is very weak, 
with the actual value (0.00023 Wm  1K  2). In contrast, for Bi88Sb12, all 
of its eight features fall within the SHAP suggested feature value range, 
which suggests that Bi88Sb12 has a strong PF value. The XGB model 
predicts a PF for Bi88Sb12 (actual PF = 0.0042 Wm  1K  2) of 0.0046 
Wm  1K  2, with a deviation of 9.5 %.
In addition, taking the four materials Bi2Te3, Sb2Te3, In0.05Co4Sb12, 
CeFe3CoSb12 [29] as an example, which are also located in the data of 
the test set, it can be seen from Fig. 12 that only the M_range_n of Sb2Te3 
is not in the range of SHAP-recommended eigenvalues, and all eigen-
values of Bi2Te3 are within the SHAP-recommended eigenvalue range 
after replacing the Sb elements in Sb2Te3 After replacing the Sb element 
in Sb2Te3 with Bi element, all the eigenvalues of Bi2Te3 are within the 
range of eigenvalues recommended by SHAP. For In0.05Co4Sb12 and 
CeFe3CoSb12, the number of features within the SHAP-recommended 
feature range is also increased from 3 to 4 by replacing the Ce and Fe 
elements with a certain amount of In elements.
According to the data analysis in Table 4, the SHAP-recommended 
range of feature values shows a positive correlation with the number 
of features, i.e., when the number of features within the recommended 
values increases, the PF values also increase. The size of the PF value can 
be effectively adjusted by regulating the material constituent elements 
and introducing an appropriate amount of additional elements. There-
fore, we can develop thermoelectric materials with excellent PF values 
based on the key feature parameters and their boundary conditions.
Combining the above methods, we aim to dope some elements from 
known high-performance thermoelectric materials and adjust their 
compositions to achieve high thermoelectric power factors and low 
thermal conductivity, thereby enhancing the thermoelectric properties 
of the materials. Our model will predict the thermoelectric property 
parameters of the new materials, which will then be validated using DFT 
to design new high-performance thermoelectric materials [64].

insufficient cell expansion, and poorly set K-point sampling can lead to 
the appearance of spurious frequencies. In addition, the absolute values 
of these imaginary frequencies are similar to the deviations when the 
dispersion curve of the acoustic branch fails to pass through the zero 
point accurately, when Newton’s third law is not applied to the force 
constant matrix. For these reasons, the instabilities in these regions are 
considered spurious rather than actual physical instabilities [66–68].

As shown in Table 7, κe increases with increasing temperature, and 
κph decreases with increasing temperature. At low temperatures, the 
electrical conductivity σ of 2D materials is low, κe= LσT (L is the Lorentz 
constant) is extremely small, which leads to the dominance of the 
phonon thermal conductivity κph. At high temperatures, the vibrational 
amplitude of atoms increases, and the phonon lifetime shortens drasti-
cally, which results in the attenuation of phonon thermal conductivity, 
and secondly, intrinsic excitations make the electrical conductivity σ 
rises substantially, at which time the electronic thermal conductivity 
κe dominates. At 300 K, the phonon thermal conductivity 
κph contributes 82.5 % of the total thermal conductivity κ, and at 
900 K, the phonon thermal conductivity κph contributes 7.5 % of the 
total thermal conductivity κ. This is in agreement with the expectation 
that the phonon thermal conductivity is dominant in typical thermo-
electric materials [72].

4. Conclusion
Our work considers six machine learning models and constructs a 
machine learning-based thermoelectric material design system through 
the steps of data acquisition, feature selection, model construction, 
model optimization, and model interpretation. The composite feature 
selection method is utilized to characterize PF and TC, respectively, and 
10 optimal features for PF and 11 optimal features for TC are filtered 
from 184 original features. Based on the subset of optimal features, the 
XGB regression model has the highest R2 scores of 0.86 for PF and 0.94 
for TC. The SHAP method is introduced to reveal some hidden infor-
mation between these features and the thermoelectric power factor. The 
results reveal the pattern of materials with good thermoelectric power 
factor: the M_min_GSp, M_range_N, D_min_v, and M_mean_e are larger 
than 18, 23.5, 8.5, and 1.78, respectively, while the D_std_r, D_std_i, 
M_max_NU, and D_std_v are smaller than 14.7, 126200, 3.5, and 5.3. 
Finally, the results of this paper are validated by using SnSe 0.25Te0.75 
material as an example. The interpretability of the method is instructive 
for the design of thermoelectric materials and can accelerate the 
development of thermoelectric materials. There is some overfitting in 
the proposed model due to the limited publicly available data, and a 
larger dataset helps to reduce this overfitting.
